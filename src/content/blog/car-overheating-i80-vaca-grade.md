Solano County summers find every weak cooling system on the road. A run of 100-degree
days, a sustained climb like the Vaca grade between Fairfield and Vacaville, stop-and-go
at the Cordelia Junction, and the air conditioning working flat out — that combination
pushes a marginal radiator, a tired water pump, or a low coolant level over the edge.
What you do in the first minute after the gauge starts climbing decides whether you're
replacing a $40 hose or an engine.

![Four steps when your car overheats: turn the air conditioning off and the heater on full, pull off at the next exit, shut the engine off and never open a hot radiator cap, then call for a tow if it overheated rather than driving on](/img/overheating-steps.svg)

## Step 1: A/C off, heater on full — even in July

It feels backwards, and it works. Your heater core is a small second radiator. Turning
the heat to maximum with the fan on high pulls heat out of the coolant and dumps it into
the cabin. At the same time, switching off the air conditioning removes the extra load
the compressor puts on the engine.

Roll the windows down and put up with it for a few miles. It can buy you enough margin
to reach an exit safely, and sometimes enough to bring the needle back down.

## Step 2: Take the next exit, don't push on

The instinct on a grade is to keep going to the top, or to a better exit, or to the gas
station you know is three miles ahead. Don't. Every minute an engine runs genuinely hot,
the aluminum cylinder head is expanding and the head gasket is taking damage.

On I-80 through our area, the exits come frequently enough that there's almost always
one within a mile or two: Air Base Parkway, Travis Boulevard, North Texas, Cherry Glen,
Alamo Drive. A parking lot off an exit is a far better place to deal with this than the
freeway shoulder, where you're standing a few feet from traffic doing 70.

If you genuinely can't make an exit, get as far right as possible, put the hazards on,
and stay belted inside the car unless you can get out on the passenger side behind a
barrier.

## Step 3: Engine off — and leave the radiator cap alone

This is the one that sends people to the emergency room. A cooling system under heat is
pressurized, and the coolant inside is well above boiling. Twisting a hot radiator cap
or the coolant reservoir cap releases that pressure all at once, and the coolant comes
out as a spray of scalding liquid and steam — straight at your face and hands.

Leave every cap on until the engine has cooled for at least thirty minutes, and even
then, cover it with a thick rag and turn it slowly. Better still, let someone with the
right gear handle it.

If you see steam, don't open the hood immediately either. Wait until it stops.

## Step 4: If it actually overheated, tow it

Here's the honest part. If the needle went into the red, or you saw steam, or a warning
light came on, the car shouldn't be driven further — even after it cools and the gauge
reads normal again.

The reason is that overheating is usually a symptom, not the problem. Something let the
coolant level drop or stopped it circulating: a split hose, a failed water pump, a stuck
thermostat, a cracked radiator. That fault is still there after the engine cools. Drive
off and the needle climbs again within a few miles, and the second overheat is the one
that warps the head.

A tow to a shop at that point is genuinely the cheapest decision you'll make all day.

## Signs you're heading toward it

Catch it early and you avoid the whole thing:

- **The gauge sitting higher than normal.** Most cars hold the needle dead center. If
  yours is drifting toward the top on hot days, something's marginal.
- **A sweet smell.** Coolant has a distinctive syrupy smell. Smelling it inside the car
  or after parking usually means a leak.
- **Puddles.** Green, orange, or pink fluid under the front of the car is coolant.
- **The A/C blowing warmer at idle.** Some cars reduce A/C load automatically when the
  engine runs hot — a subtle early warning.
- **Low coolant in the overflow tank.** Check it cold, with the engine off, once a month
  in summer.

## Why Solano County is so hard on cooling systems

Two things stack up here. The first is heat: Vacaville and Fairfield regularly see
multi-day stretches past 100°F, and the air your radiator is trying to shed heat into is
already hot. The second is grade: the climb on I-80 between Fairfield and Vacaville is
long enough and steady enough to load an engine continuously, where flat freeway driving
would let it recover.

Add commuter traffic at the Junction — crawling in stop-and-go with the A/C running and
little airflow through the radiator — and you have ideal conditions for a marginal
system to fail. It's why late July and August bring a noticeable spike in overheating
calls.
